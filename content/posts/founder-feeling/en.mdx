There's a specific kind of loneliness that comes from building alone. Not the social kind — I have friends, I have calls, I have communities. A different kind: the absence of someone who understands exactly what you're working on and why it matters.

## The problem with explaining it

You can describe your work to people. Most will be polite. Some will be curious. Almost none will get the weight of a decision you've been sitting with for three weeks.

That's not a criticism. It's just a structural reality of doing something unusual. The more specific your work, the smaller the audience that can actually follow it.

## What it costs

The cost isn't just emotional. It compounds into decisions.

When there's no one to stress-test ideas against, you stress-test them yourself — which means you're playing both sides. You argue for it, argue against it, and eventually resolve it, usually by going with your gut. Sometimes that's right. Sometimes it's just exhaustion pretending to be clarity.

## What helps

A small number of people who are doing something adjacent. Not the same thing — adjacent. They understand enough to ask the right questions, but aren't so close that their opinions are contaminated by their own context.

The other thing: writing. Not publishing — writing. The discipline of turning a half-formed thought into a sentence forces a clarity that thinking alone doesn't.

## The part nobody says out loud

There's a version of this loneliness that's actually the signal you want. If you're building something genuinely new, the absence of easy understanding is evidence you're in the right territory. It's not proof. But it's a data point.

The flip side: loneliness that comes from bad decisions is indistinguishable from the productive kind until much later.

That's the part nobody warns you about.
